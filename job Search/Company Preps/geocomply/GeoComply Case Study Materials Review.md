# GeoComply case study materials review

This is a working reference for understanding all **16 files in the supplied materials folder**, with the submitted resume, Technology graduate job description, and Eleanor correspondence kept in view. Each item has a short explanation followed by findings, limitations, and implications for later brainstorming. No project has been selected.

**Update:** The subsequently supplied [[STRETCH Case - The Table.pdf|case brief]] is now included below. Its heading covers STRETCH, FDE and Graduate Rotational. It confirms that the work must depend meaningfully on at least two objects from The Table and that the presentation is at most 15 minutes. The brief is an additional file outside the original 16-file materials directory.

The strongest theme across the pack is that GeoComply needs people who connect technical evidence to customer outcomes. A closed ticket, successful identity check, busy account, or impressive feature can still leave the actual problem unresolved. Showing day-one ownership means identifying that gap, verifying it, choosing a manageable intervention, and explaining how someone would use and evaluate it.

**How to read the evidence:** “Source” describes what a supplied file says. “Calculated” means recomputed from the supplied data. “Interpretation” means a possible explanation or implication. Most customer scenarios and all supplied datasets are synthetic. The company background and published articles provide context; their external claims have not been independently updated or verified here.

## Application context and the bar to aim for

### What Eleanor actually said

Eleanor says STRETCH and the rotational program use the same messy-problem case. The distinction is the expected level of ownership: promise for a four-month intern, versus someone trusted with real work from week one for an 18-month graduate. She will consider Smarth for both. The graduate process is more intensive and includes someone at director level.

Her figures are recruiting context from her email: 20–40 internships globally per year, three rotational participants in 2026, possibly 4–6 STRETCH slots this term, and potentially only one January rotational graduate. Past interns who have graduated receive priority. These are not a hiring guarantee or a reason to inflate the project scope.

**Interpretation:** The case needs to make judgment visible. A useful small tool with defensible evidence, clear constraints, and a credible handover could demonstrate more ownership than a broad platform whose benefits are assumed.

The supplied posting summary describes an on-site Vancouver Technology program starting January 2027, lasting 18 months, with two or three functions across home, cross-track, and landing rotations. It lists CAD $60,000–$72,000, 40 hours per week, and a November 10, 2026 deadline or 250 applications, whichever comes first. These details are recorded from the local document, not rechecked against a live posting.

For the case, the relevant requirements are owning a deliverable from discovery through use, improving inefficient processes, working with messy data, using AI while verifying its outputs, communicating with technical and commercial colleagues, and handling confidential information carefully. Rotation-end presentations make explaining impact part of the work.

## 1. About the Intern Program

[Source document](<materials/About the Intern Program.docx>)

**In brief:** A program manifesto and practical internship guide explaining what initiative looks like, how work is supported, and what evidence matters in evaluation.

The manifesto treats early talent as a future workforce investment. Interns are expected to find problems, challenge processes, start work without waiting for a perfect assignment, and own their growth. The company, program, and managers also have responsibilities: meaningful work, direction, resources, weekly feedback, and help when work is blocked. This is not a claim that an intern should operate without guidance.

The guide covers onboarding, an immersion challenge, a signed-off 30-60-90 plan, weekly check-ins and standups, buddy meetings, a co-founder conversation, a senior-leader meeting, a midpoint review, a final showcase, and knowledge transfer. Its dated calendar is for a September–December cohort; it should not be treated as the January graduate timetable.

Evaluation has four pillars: professionalism and presence, innovation and disruption, business value, and improving the team culture. Exceptional ratings require evidence such as a deliverable, screenshot, or link. Retention combines performance, a seven-minute showcase with four minutes of unscripted questions, and a business case subject to headcount and budget. Strong work alone does not guarantee a role.

**Insights for this case:** The repeated question is what changes when the work ships. Build evidence around a named user, a recurring problem, a demonstrable output, and a measurable change. The guide also expects early escalation when blocked and emphasizes named outputs from checkpoints. A handover and unresolved-questions list would support the same ownership story.

**Limitations:** Some schedule descriptions are inconsistent: the detailed calendar and summary touchpoints place leadership meetings differently. The intern guide mentions office attendance three times weekly; the graduate posting says on site. Keep the programs' arrangements separate.

## 2. GeoComply The Story So Far

[Source document](<materials/GeoComply_ The Story So Far.docx>)

**In brief:** An interview-exercise narrative about how location compliance became a broader location, device, and identity business.

The document describes GeoComply's 2011 founding, early US gaming launches, move to Vancouver, and growth as states opened sports betting. It presents fraud detection as an extension of the signals collected for location compliance, and IDComply as a response to operators wanting help with identity and age checks before a bet.

Its central strategic argument is that the early wave of state launches is slowing. The embedded chart shows six launches in 2019, one in 2025, and none through the stated 2026 reporting point. These are the document's historical framing and chart values, not an independently verified market forecast.

The new-industry challenge is different: a sportsbook has to comply with location restrictions, while many other buyers must decide whether the fraud reduction, licensing protection, or other benefit justifies cost, integration, permissions, and user friction. The same failed check may be a binding compliance outcome in one context and an input to a customer's own decision in another.

**Insights:** Product precision alone does not establish value. A useful project should identify the action a specific customer can take with the result. This connects directly to TV.NZ's reluctance to show location prompts, the pipeline's stalled integrations, and GreenTeam's disconnected identity and device views.

**Limitations:** The narrative simplifies industry rules and mixes historical public context with exercise framing. Its prediction-market discussion is not current legal advice. A check of current rules would be a separate task if we choose a regulation-dependent project.

## 3. Product Landscape Orientation

[Source document](<materials/Product Landscape Orientation.docx>)

**In brief:** A map of GeoComply's products and the tension between operator requirements and end-user experience.

The paying customer wants compliance, fraud prevention, and operational efficiency. Their user wants to complete an action with minimal effort. Verification can protect the business while causing legitimate people to leave, so pass rate should be considered alongside fraud, friction, review workload, and cost.

The document groups products into four layers:

| Layer | Role | Examples in the source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Signal capture | Gather phone, browser, location, or premises signals | Core SDKs, Player Location Check/GDK, PreCheck, BrowserGuard, PinPoint |
| Detection and decision | Interpret signals and return location, device, fraud, or identity results | Core engine, DeviceGuard, RiskGuard, GeoGuard, IDComply |
| Investigation and tooling | Help humans inspect evidence and manage cases | Hub/UBO/Explorer, PowerMap, block management, CMS |
| Compliance and business platforms | Handle adjacent regulatory and commercial workflows | OneComply and other business tools referenced in the landscape |

Core concerns location and device signals. GeoGuard detects IP obfuscation such as VPNs and proxies. IDComply orchestrates identity checks across vendors in a waterfall. OneComply manages licensing workflows. The document categorizes some offerings as standalone and others as using Core data; this is an orientation model, not a complete architecture specification.

It also compares buying priorities by industry and lists alternatives across product layers. The buying-process section matters as much as the product list: integrations require a business case, procurement, testing, and often months of work. Contracts, renewals, in-house teams, and ownership of budget can delay switching.

**Insights:** A case tool at the investigation layer can show practical value without pretending to replace GeoComply's detection engines. Conversely, a new dashboard needs to lead to a decision or action. The resume's operational interfaces and data pipelines fit this layer well.

**Definition caution:** The narrative describes pass rate in terms of legitimate people. The supplied IDComply CSV actually calculates it over **all sessions started** and does not label which starters are legitimate. Those are different measures. Also, the “Core” name is used for a suite and an engine in different places; name the specific component when making a proposal.

## 4. Quarterly Business Review transcript

[Source document](<materials/Meeting Transcript_ Quarterly Business Review Readout (Notes by Gemini).docx>)

**In brief:** A fully synthetic meeting about four forward-deployed customer pods. It is rich in unresolved problems that the opening AI summary often softens or misses.

### Betting.com

The fictional Alaska launch expects 40,000 signups in the first three months. A newly approved feature improved verification by about 9.5 percentage points in testing, but the customer declines it to preserve a consistent integration. Its finance team values a new user at about $300 in the first year.

**Illustrative calculation:** 40,000 × 9.5% = 3,800 additional verified signups; 3,800 × $300 = $1.14 million in potential first-year customer value. This assumes the testing improvement generalizes and the additional verified users have that value. It is neither booked revenue nor GeoComply revenue, and costs are not provided.

A separate self-exclusion workflow takes two people about one day a week each and leaves gaps between manual list uploads. Support closes the ticket because the product behaves as configured. The customer's work and risk remain. At 52 weeks, that is approximately 104 person-days annually, before allowing for holidays or workload changes. Salary savings cannot be calculated from the pack.

**Interpretation:** There may be both an adoption problem and a reusable product opportunity. The useful next step is to understand the integration objection and quantify repeated manual work across customers, rather than assume a feature launch resolves either.

### TV.NZ

The broadcaster uses GeoGuard but refuses a viewer location prompt because of abandonment concerns. It faces a cheaper IP-only competitor. A rights audit reportedly found about 6% of streams outside the licensed territory, largely through rented residential connections, and the next rights renewal makes leakage commercially significant.

Priya asks the team to measure actual leakage and speak with the person accountable for rights renewal. Sending another Core comparison repeats an approach that already failed.

**Interpretation:** The relevant buyer may be different from the technical champion. A solution must address leakage and the customer's friction constraint. The transcript is not evidence that every residential proxy is undetectable or that location permissions can be bypassed.

### Stawks.io

Direct engineering collaboration reduces location prompts from 22% to 9%. The source says each ten percentage points of prompting costs two or three points of abandonment. A linear illustration suggests roughly 2.6–3.9 percentage points of abandonment improvement from a 13-point prompt reduction, but no measured before-and-after conversion result is supplied.

The June 9 latency spike is already explained by the customer's planned load test. Changes that improved prompting are only partly captured in Slack and Jira. There is another exclusion-list request and a CEO request for expansion into two countries that has not been followed up.

**Interpretation:** Reusable integration knowledge and prompt follow-up can have direct value. An alert without event context creates avoidable work. Expansion is an actionable commercial signal, not a routine item to leave unowned.

### GreenTeam Health

High product usage makes the account look healthy. Yet fake member accounts pass document checks while location and device behavior looks suspicious. Identity and security teams use separate dashboards, and overnight bot attacks create thousands of attempts. The customer may also pay another device vendor; the data scientist explicitly says to understand dependencies before recommending removal.

A new CFO is reviewing vendors with a 15% spending-reduction brief. GeoComply has not met that decision maker and relies on a security champion to explain its value.

**Interpretation:** Adoption, fraud effectiveness, and renewal health are different things. Combined evidence and a clear explanation of business value could matter more than another usage score. The Signal Explorer health accounts provide a related, later exercise dataset, but are not transaction-level proof of this June meeting's events.

### Problems in the AI readout

The opening notes label GreenTeam healthy, leave the latency spike as an investigation, and recommend another TV.NZ comparison. The actual transcript contains renewal risk, an explained load test, and an explicit rejection of another comparison as the next move. It also contains upstream actions missing from the summarized next steps.

**Case implication:** Read the transcript before trusting the summary. A useful extracted action needs the supporting passage, owner, business consequence, and current status. Customer names vary between Stawks, Stocks, and Stocks.io; aliases would need confirmation before any cross-file join.

## 5. Technical Support Shadowing transcript

[Source document](<materials/Meeting Transcript_ Technical Support Shadowing (Notes by Gemini).docx>)

**In brief:** An adapted onboarding session about how support translates customer requests, coordinates changes, and copes with missing product knowledge.

The source describes twelve people providing 24-hour coverage and a meaningful response within 24 hours. That response can be a resolution or an honest estimate; it is not a promise to resolve every request within 24 hours. Shared customer Slack channels feed CRM tickets through an existing “swarm” integration. The reason is durable records: the exercise says Slack messages disappear after a year, whereas compliance questions may arise much later.

The Harbourline request involves checking old PlayNorth Westmark integration details and adding the operator to an organization. It requires CSM approval, legal approval, a Jira request to deployment coordinators, changes in both staging and production, screenshot evidence, and a complete answer to the original customer question. This process already has coordination tools; duplicating Slack-to-ticket creation would miss the gap.

Customer notes can impose additional restrictions. For example, the transcript says one customer's player-facing text requires regulator approval. Support must inspect those notes before acting. A useful automation could assemble context, check completeness, and draft a handoff while retaining required approvals and authorized execution.

The IDComply examples expose knowledge problems:

- **Ashgrove:** An ambiguous date is interpreted using a different date convention. Broad AI search finds nothing because the relevant integration guidance is in an old email. The proposed remedies include documenting the format, rejecting ambiguous input clearly, and giving support read-only visibility into relevant settings.
- **Marigold:** A typo in staging returns failure because the test vendor requires exact matching. A partial result requires a special preset profile. The docs only say to use test data, so the customer cannot infer the correct method.
- **Customer health:** An intern is already working on a unified IDComply health view because customers sometimes report declining pass rates before GeoComply notices. Support handles about fifty IDComply tickets weekly in this adapted scenario, and subject-matter experts still have the general queue.

**Insights:** Better retrieval cannot recover knowledge that has never been captured. Knowledge extraction would need source references, review, customer/environment scope, and an update path. The most useful output might be a verified testing guide or a complete escalation packet rather than a free-form assistant.

**Evidence cautions:** Gemini attributes Ashgrove's problem to vendor settings and says the product manager supplied the answer. The dialogue does not establish that account of the resolution. It points to an emailed integration guide. The date example itself is inconsistent: reading November 5 as May 11 makes a person older, not younger, so it does not by itself explain the claimed underage rejection. The September heading also conflicts with January/Christmas remarks. Preserve the underlying ambiguity problem without treating the example's chronology or exact cause as proven.

## 6. Match Ready article

[Saved article](<materials/Match Ready_ How GeoComply prepares for Major Sporting Events _ GeoComply.html>) — dated July 8, 2026 in the supplied copy.

**In brief:** A published DevOps interview explaining how GeoComply prepares for sudden sports-event demand and a long tournament's cost profile.

The article reports 81.4 million checks on Super Bowl game day, 145.6 million across Final Four weekend, and 1.94 billion in the World Cup's first four weeks. These cover different time windows and cannot be compared as peak throughput. Its engineering point is the worst second: demand can jump tenfold immediately, too quickly for reactive scaling alone.

Preparation combines customer campaign expectations, internal product/infrastructure changes, historical benchmarks, testing, advance capacity, and buffers. Stateless application layers can scale quickly; database changes need longer planning. Super Bowl operation reportedly uses about three times the normal instance count. Each environment needs its own verification that planned capacity and alerts were actually applied.

The article describes early utilization alerts below 50%, multiple availability zones, database standby failover, a queuing layer, and disaster recovery. A six-gate World Cup process uses observed demand to adjust capacity between stages instead of sustaining peak provisioning for six weeks.

**Insights:** Readiness is observable evidence of capacity, dependencies, communications, and open issues. This fits the resume's monitoring and incident work. The QBR's customer load test is a useful companion example: monitoring needs expected-event context.

**Limits:** This is a company narrative, not raw telemetry or an independently measured uptime study. It does not provide per-second traffic, latency distributions, capacity curves, cloud costs, or a formal service objective. Its database and autoscaling descriptions are specific to its account of these systems. A readiness prototype could organize evidence; it could not validate actual capacity using this pack alone.

## 7. Pig butchering white paper

[Source PDF](<materials/R3_WP_Pig-Butchering_2024.pdf>) — ten pages, published with a 2024 copyright.

**In brief:** A GeoComply argument for using multiple location and device signals to interrupt relationship-based investment scams before and during the movement of money.

Pages 3–5 explain the scam, distinguish manipulation from payment movement, and describe concentrated facilities that may use trafficked people. Blockchain investigation follows funds; the paper argues that communication platforms can also prevent contact originating from known or newly detected facilities.

Pages 6–8 describe three signals: activity in higher-risk jurisdictions, unusual geographic concentrations of accounts, and extensive location obfuscation. The maps contrast a distant apparent IP location with Wi-Fi-derived locations near facilities. The paper argues for combining IP with GPS, Wi-Fi, and cellular evidence rather than trusting IP alone.

Pages 9–10 discuss account sharing, implausible travel, changing access patterns, and remote-desktop use during financial activity. A valid identity or expected device location does not establish that the account holder controls a transaction.

**Insights:** The transferable idea is combining evidence and investigating alternatives. A campus network can create a large benign concentration; a verified health identity can coexist with suspicious shared-device behavior. Those are precisely the two contrasting sandbox examples.

**Limits:** The paper's loss estimates, prevalence figures, and regulatory references are attributed historical claims, not new findings from our data. It supplies neither a labeled training set nor a detection evaluation. The Ohio sandbox does not demonstrate a scam compound. Geography, VPN use, or remote desktop alone should not be treated as proof of crime, and account users in a scam facility may themselves be coerced victims.

## 8. Vertical Pipeline Extract workbook

[Source workbook](<materials/Vertical_Pipeline_Extract_SYNTHETIC.xlsx>) — two visible sheets, no formulas or charts.

**In brief:** A synthetic sales view showing where demand is shifting and why demand does not always become a purchase.

### New Vertical Opps

Rows 6–23 contain **18 selected opportunities**, not the complete pipeline. Fields cover vertical, prospect profile, opening quarter, acquisition source, stated problem, products, furthest stage, status, time open, ACV band, and the last rep note. ACV is an annual contract value band; dollar signs are categories, not numerical prices.

Several patterns recur. Technical champions cannot overcome a repeated security review by themselves. Health prospects can stall when compliance and product each expect the other to pay. Dating, marketplace, and streaming prospects resist permission prompts. A locked-down exam browser creates a six-week integration requirement. A gig platform requests one API and receives four documents. A ticketing buyer chooses a specialist because it does not view GeoComply as an anti-bot vendor.

The wins are instructive too: a crypto customer expands to two more markets and adds identity; a lender adds location after seeing device signals; a streaming rights contract creates urgency; a benefits buyer wants to reuse an existing health implementation. An online-safety pilot has unclear ownership after the pilot.

Four selected opportunities cite AI answers as the acquisition source, called AEO in the sheet. They illustrate discoverability, not conversion performance. The donation example wants immediate self-service purchasing and chooses another vendor despite being marked active.

### Industry Growth

The synthetic non-gaming share of all opportunities rises from **20.4% in FY24 to 33.3% in FY25 to 51.1% in FY26 YTD**. Core gaming's share moves from 79.6% to 48.9%. The sheet reports non-gaming opportunity growth of 71.4% and won-deal growth of 153.8% from FY25 to FY26, but FY26 is year to date, so exposure periods require clarification before interpreting this as a comparable annual trend.

Among won non-gaming deals, the first product discussed shifts from location at 75.0% in FY24 to 24.2% in FY26 YTD. Identity grows from 0% to 45.5%; fraud is 30.3% in FY26. These are shares of **won deals by starting conversation**, not product revenue, market share, or win rates.

**Insights:** The opportunity is often packaging, ownership, proof of value, procurement, or reusable implementation—not missing detection capability. The repeat health setup and crypto expansion suggest that making a successful integration repeatable could matter.

**Limits and inconsistencies:** Dollar amounts, deal counts for the full pipeline, fiscal-year boundaries, and costs are absent. NV-184 is 22 days open but says there has been no reply for six weeks. NV-191 is active even though the note says it chose another vendor. NV-152 records an expansion that resolves the older NV-015 note about expansion being postponed. Use opportunity IDs, account history, and dated evidence before treating current status as reliable. AEO's four examples cannot establish channel return on investment.

## 10. IDComply verification funnel CSV

[Source CSV](<materials/idcomply_case_pack [SYNTHETIC]/idcomply_verification_funnel_SYNTHETIC_v2.csv>)

**In brief:** Weekly verification outcomes for eight customers across 26 Mondays, February 2–July 27, 2026. One row is a customer, jurisdiction, and reporting week.

The 208 rows cover Rivet Play in Ontario, Ashgrove Bet in the UK, and six US customers: Tidewater, Cobalt, Marigold, Foundry, Selkirk, and Pinnacle. Fields track sessions started, data submitted, data-only passes, requests for documents, uploads, document passes, final verification passes, manual-review queues, and pass rate.

**Calculated totals:** 950,604 sessions started; 883,142 submitted data; 647,205 final passes. The volume-weighted pass rate is **68.08%**, calculated as total final passes divided by total starts. Averaging customer percentages equally would answer a different question.

Documents were requested for 291,972 sessions; 158,860 uploaded them. That is **54.41% upload completion**, leaving 133,112 requested-document sessions without an upload. Of uploaded documents, 84.44% passed. This suggests an important pre-upload investigation, but the aggregate data does not explain why people failed to upload or distinguish abandonment from technical failure.

| Customer | Full-period pass rate | Pattern worth examining |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Rivet Play | 65.42% | Monthly weighted rate falls from 72.05% in February to 58.99% in July |
| Tidewater Casino | 65.78% | Persistently lower rates, around 65–67% by month |
| Foundry Gaming | 65.19% | Persistently lower rates, around 64–66% by month |
| Cobalt Sportsbook | 69.66% | April weighted rate 52.76%, versus 72.29% in March and 72.94% in May |
| Marigold Gaming | 70.68% | April weighted rate 59.96%, with two weekly values near 47% |
| Selkirk Interactive | 70.60% | April weighted rate 60.77%, with two weekly values below 50% |
| Ashgrove Bet | 71.19% | Week of March 16 falls to 39.46%, a sharp isolated exception |
| Pinnacle Play | 72.84% | Relatively stable monthly results, useful as context rather than a universal target |

Monthly figures group complete weekly rows by their week-start month; they are not exact calendar-month activity totals.

**Insights:** A single ranking hides slow deterioration, sudden incidents, and persistently weak conversion. A health view should distinguish those patterns and show the stage associated with a change. Rivet's document-upload completion drops from 73.76% in February to 50.10% in July, alongside its pass-rate decline. That is a lead to investigate, not proof of the cause.

**Limits:** No user-level event sequence, integration-change dates, rejection reasons, or confirmed legitimate-user labels. Data-only passes plus document passes exceed final passes; the supplied definition does not fully explain the final-decision reconciliation. Keep the supplied final-pass count authoritative and the unexplained difference visible rather than force a simple additive funnel. Manual-review outcomes and timing are also absent.

## 12. IDComply vendor funnel CSV

[Source CSV](<materials/idcomply_case_pack [SYNTHETIC]/idcomply_vendor_funnel_weekly_SYNTHETIC.csv>)

**In brief:** Vendor-level outcomes and spend for each customer/week, with two different cohorts that must remain separate.

There are **1,092 live waterfall rows** and **962 calibration rows**. Each row records vendor, step, checks sent, matches, no matches, timeouts, and USD cost. Step 0 is sanctions, step 9 is document step-up, and intermediate steps vary by customer. Calibration step numbers are intentionally blank.

In the live waterfall, later vendors receive a selected population left over from earlier checks. For example, Rivet uses V02, then V03, then V04; Ashgrove uses V03 then V04. Comparing their raw live match percentages as if they received interchangeable applicants would be misleading.

Calibration sends a common sample to the eligible candidate vendors. It includes an `ANY_REGISTRY` roll-up, not a seventh vendor. It has zero cost in those roll-up rows and must not be counted as an additional actual check purchase or unique sample population.

**Calculated findings:** Live waterfall spend is **$603,307.33**; calibration spend is **$29,862.25**, separately. V02's live timeout rate is **8.08%**, compared with 1.20% for V01 and 0.61% for V03. These are observed cohorts, not controlled estimates of the effect of switching. V03 accounts for $228,171.38 of live spend and documents for $190,632.00, making both important to a cost discussion.

Useful reconciliations hold for all 208 customer-weeks: V06 checks equal data submitted; V05 checks equal document uploads; V05 matches equal document passes; and live V01–V04 matches sum to data-only passes. Every vendor row reconciles checks to matches plus no matches plus timeouts.

**Insights:** This supports explaining where spend and failures occur and developing a testable routing hypothesis. Calibration is more suitable for comparing candidate coverage than live residual cohorts.

**Limits:** Aggregate calibration results do not identify which individual applicants matched which combination of vendors. Adding vendor match rates double-counts overlap. The supplied roll-up gives one aggregate union measure, not every possible pair or sequence. Exact savings, a globally optimal ordering, and the effect on final fraud cannot be established without additional assumptions or applicant-level experiments. Mandatory screening and regional coverage must remain constraints.

### Campus network example

IP `164.107.92.18`, named `CampusResNet`, has **373 checks from 40 accounts on 40 devices across 30 access points**, all passing, between 17:10 and 21:48 UTC on October 10. Embedded signup metadata spans August 2024 to March 2026; none of those accounts is in the supplied operator reports.

**Interpretation:** This is consistent with shared campus infrastructure rather than forty people on one device. It supplies substantial alternative context to the Copilot's suggestion of coordinated fraud. It does not prove every account benign, and absence from a report is not a clean ground-truth label.

### Linked health accounts example

GreenTeam account `GTH-418864` has a verified identity and six passes among eight checks. Device `49031dd358f67b91` connects it to **two other GreenTeam identities and two Betting.com accounts**. Both betting accounts have operator report categories of suspected identity theft, although their free-text suspension reasons are blank.

**Interpretation:** A high-priority investigation should combine new identities, shared-device links, related operator reports, timing, precision, and financial action. A verified identity and a majority of passing checks do not justify calling this low risk. Cross-operator links are available in this exercise; a real product would require appropriate access and data-sharing rules.

### Data quality that affects conclusions

The timestamps run from October 5 at 13:50 UTC through **October 12 at 09:27 UTC**. There are 150 checks after the advertised week ends in Ohio time. Do not silently discard them or label the entire extract October 5–11 without stating a filter.

The trust Boolean and text reason conflict on 149 rows: 96 say trusted with a low-confidence reason, and 53 say not trusted with reason `Trusted`. They need separate treatment until semantics or data quality is clarified. Coordinates, city labels, status, and uncertainty are also different pieces of evidence; rounded map points are not enough to override a supplied jurisdiction result.

Blank Wi-Fi fields for cellular connections and blank financial fields for betting checks are expected, not automatically missing-data defects. No stakes, losses, or final withdrawal settlement results are supplied.
